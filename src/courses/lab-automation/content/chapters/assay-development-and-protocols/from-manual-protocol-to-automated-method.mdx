Everything in this course so far has assumed a method already exists, ready to run on an automated system. This chapter is about how that method actually gets created — starting with the gap between a protocol written for a human and one a machine can execute.

### A manual protocol speaks to a person, not a machine

A manual protocol is written the way a person reads instructions: "add 50 µL of reagent to each well, then incubate for 30 minutes at room temperature." A person reading this fills in an enormous amount of implicit knowledge — how to hold the pipette, how to recognize "room temperature," what to do if a well looks off. None of that implicit knowledge transfers to a machine. An automated method has to make every one of those assumptions explicit: exactly which liquid class to use for the reagent, exactly which incubator and exactly what setpoint counts as the target temperature, exactly what "looks off" would even mean to something without eyes.

### Translating, not just transcribing

Turning a manual protocol into an automated method is not a word-for-word conversion — it's a translation between two fundamentally different ways of specifying work. "Mix gently" becomes a specific pipetting speed and a specific number of mix cycles. "Let it sit until the color develops" becomes a fixed timing window, because a machine can't watch for a color change unless a plate reader is explicitly built into the method to check for it. Some manual steps translate cleanly; others reveal that the original protocol was relying on a person's judgment in a way that has no obvious automated equivalent at all, and the protocol itself has to change to accommodate that.

### Where the science lives versus where the engineering lives

A useful way to separate the work: the *science* of a protocol — why this reagent, why this concentration, why this incubation time — doesn't change when it's automated. The *engineering* — how the reagent is dispensed, how the incubation is timed and verified, how the result is captured — is entirely new work layered on top of an unchanged scientific core. Confusing the two causes real problems: changing the science to make automation easier corrupts the assay; refusing to change the engineering (insisting the automated system move exactly like a person's hand) often makes automation needlessly difficult or impossible.

### Why this always requires a person who understands both sides

Because this translation touches both the biology and the mechanics, it's rarely something either a bench scientist or an automation engineer can do entirely alone. A protocol translated by someone who doesn't understand the underlying chemistry risks automating a version of the assay that no longer works scientifically; one translated by someone who doesn't understand the equipment risks a method that's technically faithful to the paper protocol but impossible to actually execute reliably on real hardware.

The rest of this chapter breaks this translation into its concrete pieces — starting with the most basic one: decomposing a protocol into individual, machine-executable steps.
