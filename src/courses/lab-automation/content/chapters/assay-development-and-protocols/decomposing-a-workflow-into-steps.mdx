Translating a protocol's language, as the previous section covered, is only half the job. The other half is structural: breaking a continuous procedure into the discrete, individually executable steps an automated system actually runs.

### A protocol is a paragraph; a method is a list

A written protocol often describes a procedure in flowing, connected prose — several actions bundled into one sentence, conditions and exceptions woven through the narrative. An automated method needs the opposite: a strict, ordered list of atomic operations, each one a single, unambiguous instruction to a specific instrument. Decomposition is the process of pulling that list out of the prose — identifying every discrete action buried in a sentence like "wash the plate three times, then add substrate and read after 15 minutes" (three wash cycles, a dispense step, a fixed-duration wait, a read step — four operations, not one).

### Where to draw the line between one step and the next

Not every decomposition is equally useful. A step defined too coarsely ("prepare the plate") hides several distinct operations behind one label, making it impossible for a scheduler to reason about timing or resources within it. A step defined too finely (breaking a single pipetting motion into "lower the pipette," "aspirate," "raise the pipette" as three separate scheduled steps) adds bookkeeping overhead without adding any real control. The useful boundary is usually the smallest unit that a scheduler might need to reason about independently — something that has its own timing constraint, its own resource requirement, or its own possible failure mode worth tracking on its own.

### Steps need inputs, outputs, and preconditions

Beyond just ordering, each decomposed step needs to be defined with what it requires before it can run (the right plate present, the right reagent loaded) and what it produces when it's done (a plate now washed, a value now read). This is what lets the scheduling concepts from the earlier chapter — sequence dependencies, timing windows, resource requirements — actually attach to something concrete. A step with no defined precondition can't be checked before it runs; a step with no defined output gives the next step nothing to depend on.

### Decomposition reveals hidden assumptions

The act of decomposing a protocol often surfaces gaps the original manual version never had to address, because a person filled them in without noticing. "Wash the plate" implicitly assumes a specific number of wash cycles and a specific residual volume a technician would just eyeball — decomposing it into an explicit step forces someone to actually specify those numbers, sometimes for the first time. This is frequently where automating a protocol first exposes that it was never as precisely defined as everyone assumed.

With a protocol broken into discrete, schedulable steps, the next section covers a different kind of change automation often invites: shrinking and refining those steps to make the whole assay faster and cheaper to run.
