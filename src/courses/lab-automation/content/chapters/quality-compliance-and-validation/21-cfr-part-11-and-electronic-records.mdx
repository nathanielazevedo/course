GxP establishes the general expectation that work must be documented and traceable. **21 CFR Part 11** is the specific U.S. FDA regulation that says what that means when the documentation itself is electronic rather than paper — which, in an automated lab, is almost always the case.

### The core question Part 11 answers

Before electronic records were common, "documentation" meant a signed, dated, physical page that was hard to alter without leaving evidence. Part 11 exists to answer a direct question: how does an electronic record — a database entry, a log file, a captured instrument result — earn that same level of trust, given that electronic data can be edited, in principle, without leaving any visible trace at all? The regulation's requirements are all downstream of that one concern.

### The core requirements

- **Audit trails.** Every creation, modification, or deletion of a record must be automatically logged — who did it, what changed, and when — in a way the user cannot turn off or edit. This is covered in depth in the next section, but it's worth noting here as the mechanism Part 11 leans on most directly.
- **Electronic signatures.** An electronic signature has to be uniquely tied to one individual, require that individual's own authentication (not shared credentials), and carry the same legal weight as a handwritten signature — meaning it can't be replicated or reused by someone else.
- **System validation.** The software generating and storing these records must itself be validated to reliably do what it claims — the same validation concept from the assay-development chapter, applied here to the record-keeping software rather than the assay.
- **Access controls.** Only authorized individuals can create, modify, or view records, enforced by the system itself rather than by policy alone.

### Why automated systems have an obligation here, not just an advantage

An automated system generates electronic records constantly — every barcode scan, every instrument reading, every scheduling decision from earlier chapters in this course. That volume of automatic record generation is an advantage for compliance in principle, but it also means Part 11's requirements apply directly and continuously to the system's own infrastructure: the driver logging an instrument's response, the scheduler recording a step's completion, and the LIMS storing a final result all need to meet these requirements, not just the final report a person eventually reads.

### Why "electronic" doesn't mean "automatically compliant"

It's a common misconception that any digital record is inherently more trustworthy than paper. A database entry with no audit trail, editable by anyone with access, offers *less* assurance than a signed paper form, precisely because a digital edit can be invisible where a paper correction leaves a visible mark. Part 11 exists specifically because electronic records needed their own, stricter standard to actually earn the trust paper records had by default — which is why simply digitizing a process doesn't satisfy the regulation on its own.

Electronic records establish what must be captured and how. The next section goes deeper on the single mechanism that makes those records trustworthy after the fact: the audit trail.
