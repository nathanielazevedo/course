Electronic records and qualified instruments both need one more thing to actually be trusted over time: proof that nothing about them was altered improperly after the fact. That proof is the audit trail, and the broader property it protects is called data integrity.

### What an audit trail actually records

An **audit trail** is an automatic, tamper-evident log of every meaningful action taken on a record: who created it, who modified it and when, what the value was before and after a change, and — critically — who *viewed* it, in systems where even access needs to be tracked. The defining property of a real audit trail is that it cannot be turned off, edited, or deleted by the same user whose actions it's recording; if a user could quietly edit the log of their own edits, the log would prove nothing.

### Data integrity: the property the audit trail exists to protect

**Data integrity** is the broader assurance that a record is accurate, complete, and reflects what actually happened — often summarized by the acronym **ALCOA** (Attributable, Legible, Contemporaneous, Original, Accurate). An audit trail is the specific mechanism that makes several of these properties verifiable after the fact: attributable (who did it — recorded in the trail), contemporaneous (recorded at the time it happened, not reconstructed later), and original (the trail preserves the actual first-recorded value, not just whatever the record currently shows).

### Why this matters more, not less, in an automated system

It might seem like automation reduces data integrity risk simply by removing manual transcription — and it does remove *that* specific risk, as covered in the data-capture chapter. But automation introduces its own integrity questions: can a person with system access silently edit a captured result before it reaches the LIMS? Does the scheduler's own log of what ran, when, get the same tamper-evident treatment as the scientific results themselves? A system can automate the science flawlessly while still leaving its own administrative records vulnerable to exactly the kind of undetected alteration Part 11 and audit trails exist to prevent.

### What a broken audit trail actually costs

A gap or inconsistency in an audit trail has the same practical effect as a gap in the chain of custody covered earlier: it doesn't just cast doubt on the one record it touches, it undermines confidence in every record generated by that system during the period the gap can't be explained. This is why audit trail integrity is treated as a foundational requirement rather than a nice-to-have feature — the trail isn't just documentation about the data, in a regulated context it effectively *is* part of what makes the data trustworthy at all.

Audit trails prove what happened to a record after it was created. The final section of this chapter covers what happens *before* a change is made at all — the deliberate process of managing and documenting change itself.
