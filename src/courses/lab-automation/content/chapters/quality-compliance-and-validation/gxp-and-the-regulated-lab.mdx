Everything covered so far in this course has been about making automation work well technically — accurate, reliable, fast. In a regulated lab, working well isn't enough on its own; the lab also has to *prove* it works well, to a standard an outside regulator can verify. That proof obligation is what GxP is about.

### What GxP actually means

**GxP** is an umbrella term for a family of "good practice" regulations — **GLP** (Good Laboratory Practice), **GMP** (Good Manufacturing Practice), **GCP** (Good Clinical Practice) — each governing a different stage of getting a product (usually a drug or medical device) from research to market. The "x" stands in for whichever practice applies to the specific stage of work; the common thread across all of them is the same underlying expectation: work has to be done consistently, documented thoroughly, and be traceable back to exactly how and by whom it was performed.

### Why GxP changes what "correct" means

Outside a regulated context, a result is correct if it's scientifically accurate — the assay measured what it claims to measure. Inside a GxP environment, a result also has to be **defensible**: not just correct, but demonstrably produced by a documented, validated process that a regulator or auditor could review months or years later and reconstruct exactly what happened. A perfectly accurate result produced by an undocumented, unvalidated process can still fail a GxP audit, because the standard isn't only "is this true," it's "can you prove this is true, to someone who wasn't there."

### Why automation is a natural fit for GxP, but not automatically compliant

Automated systems, in principle, are well suited to this kind of proof: they execute the same documented steps every time, and can log every action as it happens rather than relying on a person's memory or handwritten notes after the fact. But automation doesn't earn GxP compliance simply by existing — a system has to be deliberately built and operated to actually produce that documentation, log those actions completely, and make that record available for review. An automated system run carelessly is just as capable of failing a GxP audit as a manual process run carelessly; the technology creates the opportunity for compliance, it doesn't guarantee it.

### Why this chapter comes last in the course

Every topic covered earlier in this course — instrument control, scheduling, data capture, error recovery, maintenance — becomes the actual substance a GxP audit examines. A regulator reviewing an automated method isn't checking the biology twice; they're checking whether the engineering behind it (the very engineering this course has covered) was itself documented, controlled, and verifiable. GxP is less a separate technical topic and more a lens applied over everything already covered, which is why this final chapter revisits several earlier ideas — electronic records, validation, audit trails, change control — specifically through that lens.
