Every previous section in this chapter has been about proving something is trustworthy: the equipment, the records, the audit trail. This final section is about what happens *before* anything changes at all — the deliberate discipline that keeps a validated, qualified, documented system from quietly drifting out of the state it was proven to be in.

### Why any change is a threat to everything already proven

By this point in the course, a real pattern should be familiar: a method was validated (assay-development chapter), an instrument was qualified (previous section), and both records are protected by an audit trail. Every one of those proofs is a statement about a *specific* configuration — this exact method, on this exact instrument, with this exact software version. Change any part of that configuration, and the proof no longer automatically applies to whatever exists afterward, even if the change seems small or clearly beneficial. **Change control** is the formal process that keeps this from happening by accident.

### What change control actually requires

A change-controlled environment doesn't forbid change — it requires that change be deliberate, reviewed, and recorded rather than informal. A typical change control process requires: a documented request describing exactly what's changing and why, an assessment of what else that change could affect (does updating this driver's version affect the scheduler's timing assumptions? does a new reagent lot require re-validation?), approval from someone with the authority to weigh that impact, and a record of the decision and its reasoning that becomes part of the system's permanent history.

### Why impact assessment is the hardest step

The genuinely difficult part of change control isn't the paperwork — it's accurately identifying everything a change could actually affect, especially in an automated system where components interact in ways covered throughout this course: a scheduler's timing assumptions, a driver's expected instrument responses, a method's validated parameters. A change assessed narrowly ("this only updates the reader's firmware") can miss a real consequence ("the firmware update also changed the reader's response timing, which the driver's status-polling logic was tuned around"). This is exactly why change control review typically involves people with a broad view across the system, not just whoever is making the specific change.

### Documentation as the connective tissue across this whole chapter

Every mechanism covered in this chapter — GxP's general expectation, Part 11's electronic record requirements, IQ/OQ/PQ, audit trails — ultimately depends on documentation existing and being accurate. Change control is where that documentation is actively kept in sync with reality, rather than slowly diverging from it as the system evolves. A system with excellent original documentation that never gets updated as changes accumulate ends up, after enough time, effectively undocumented — the paperwork exists, but it no longer describes the system actually running.

### Why this closes the course

This course began by describing lab automation as a chain of interacting parts — hardware, control, orchestration — layered on top of real laboratory science. Change control is the discipline that keeps that entire chain provably trustworthy over its whole working life, not just on the day it was first built and validated. Every earlier chapter — liquid handling, instruments, robotics, scheduling, data, reliability — described how to build and run an automated lab well; this final chapter described how to prove, and keep proving, that it actually is what it claims to be.
