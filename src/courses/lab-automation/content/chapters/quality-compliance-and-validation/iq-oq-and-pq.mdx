Validation, back in the assay-development chapter, was about proving a *method* measures what it claims to. Before a method can even be validated, the *equipment* it runs on has to be validated first — proof that the hardware itself is installed correctly, operates correctly, and performs correctly for its intended use. That three-stage proof is IQ, OQ, and PQ.

### Installation Qualification: is it installed correctly?

**IQ** confirms that a piece of equipment was delivered, installed, and configured exactly as specified — the right model, the right software version, connected correctly, positioned correctly, powered and networked as required. IQ doesn't test whether the instrument *works*, only whether it's physically and configurably present the way it's supposed to be. An instrument that fails IQ hasn't necessarily malfunctioned — it might simply be wired wrong, running an unapproved software version, or missing a required accessory.

### Operational Qualification: does it work correctly, in general?

**OQ** tests the instrument's core functions across its full operating range, independent of any specific assay — does the thermal cycler actually reach and hold its specified temperatures, does the liquid handler deliver volumes within its specified accuracy tolerance, does the reader detect a known reference standard correctly. OQ answers "does this instrument do what its specification claims it can do," using generic, instrument-level tests rather than any particular protocol.

### Performance Qualification: does it work correctly, for this specific use?

**PQ** is the most specific stage: does the instrument perform correctly running the *actual* method it will be used for, under the *actual* conditions of real use. This is where instrument qualification meets the method validation from the assay-development chapter — PQ often runs the specific validated method itself, on this specific piece of qualified equipment, confirming that the combination of instrument and method together produces the expected result.

### Why the three stages have to happen in this order

Testing operational function on an instrument that hasn't passed IQ risks discovering a problem that's actually just a wiring or configuration error, wasting time chasing a phantom performance issue. Testing a specific method's performance (PQ) on an instrument that hasn't passed OQ risks the reverse problem — a method appears to fail when the real cause is a general instrument malfunction that has nothing to do with that specific method at all. Each stage exists to rule out a category of problem before the next stage's more specific, more expensive testing begins.

### Requalification: this isn't a one-time stamp either

Like method validation, IQ/OQ/PQ isn't a permanent certification earned once and never revisited. A significant repair, a relocation, a major software update, or simply the passage of a scheduled interval typically triggers **requalification** — rerunning some or all of these stages to confirm the instrument still meets the standard it originally passed. This mirrors the same principle from method validation: any meaningful change is a claim that needs its own evidence, not an assumption that yesterday's qualification still applies today.
