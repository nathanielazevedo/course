Barcodes and chain-of-custody records generate a constant stream of facts about samples — what they are, where they've been, what's happened to them. All of that information has to live somewhere, be queryable, and stay trustworthy over the life of a project. That's the job of two related but distinct systems: the LIMS and the ELN.

### LIMS: the system of record for samples

A **Laboratory Information Management System (LIMS)** is the database, and the software around it, that tracks samples through their entire lifecycle — registration, every location and transformation from the chain of custody, every result generated, and its current status. When a barcode is scanned anywhere in an automated workflow, it's very often the LIMS being queried or updated behind the scenes: looking up what that sample is, or recording what just happened to it. A LIMS is built around structured, consistent records — the same fields, the same format, for every sample of a given type — because that consistency is what makes it possible to search, filter, and report across thousands of samples reliably.

### ELN: the record of what a scientist did and decided

An **Electronic Lab Notebook (ELN)** serves a different purpose: it's where a scientist records the *narrative* of an experiment — why a protocol was chosen, what was observed, what was concluded, alongside less structured content like photos, sketches, and free-text notes. Where a LIMS wants every record to look the same, an ELN is deliberately open-ended, because the reasoning behind an experiment often can't be forced into a fixed set of fields without losing something important.

### Why both are needed, and why they're linked

Neither system alone captures everything a lab needs to remember. A LIMS without an ELN has perfect tracking of *what* happened to every sample but no record of *why* a scientist made the choices they did along the way. An ELN without a LIMS has rich context but no reliable, structured way to answer "where is sample 4471 right now" across thousands of entries. In practice, the two are usually linked — an ELN entry references the specific LIMS sample IDs it discusses, so a scientist's narrative reasoning and the system's structured tracking data can each be found starting from the other.

### Why structure and openness can't be merged into one system

It's tempting to imagine a single system doing both jobs, but the requirements actively conflict: the rigid structure a LIMS needs to make thousands of records searchable and comparable is the same rigidity that would strip an ELN entry of the nuance a scientist needs to record. Keeping them as separate, linked systems lets each optimize for what it's actually for, rather than compromising both to be adequate at everything.
