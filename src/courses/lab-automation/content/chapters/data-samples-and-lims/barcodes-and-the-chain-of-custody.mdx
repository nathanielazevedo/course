Sample identity was defined in the previous section as something that must survive every physical transformation. Barcoding — introduced earlier as a way to tell labware apart — is the mechanism that actually makes that survival possible in an automated system, and the record it produces is called the chain of custody.

### From labware identity to sample identity

Earlier, barcoding was framed around identifying a *plate*. The same mechanism extends to identifying a *sample*: each well, tube, or aliquot can be assigned its own identifier (sometimes the plate's barcode plus a well position, sometimes a fully independent code) that stays linked to that sample's identity regardless of which physical container it currently sits in. This is what lets a system say "this specific well, on this specific plate, holds this specific sample" — and keep that statement true even after the plate has been moved, split, or combined.

### Chain of custody: a record of every handoff

The **chain of custody** is the continuous, timestamped record of every time a sample changed hands or location — which instrument touched it, which person or system moved it, when it was transferred into a new container. In regulated or forensic contexts, chain of custody isn't a convenience, it's often a legal requirement: a result can be challenged and discarded if the chain of custody has a gap, because a gap means there's a window where the sample's identity or integrity can't actually be verified.

### Why automation makes this easier, not harder — if built correctly

A person handling a sample manually has to remember to log every handoff, which is exactly the kind of tedious, easy-to-skip record-keeping that gets forgotten under time pressure. An automated system, by contrast, can log every scan and every transfer automatically as a side effect of the barcode verification already covered — the same scan that confirms "this is the right plate" before a step also produces the timestamped record that the chain of custody needs. The chain of custody isn't extra work bolted onto automation; it's a natural byproduct of doing barcode verification properly at every step.

### What breaks a chain of custody

A gap appears whenever a transfer happens without a corresponding scan — a person manually moving a tube between racks without re-scanning it, a step in a method that was configured to skip verification for speed. Each of these gaps means the system's confidence in "this is still the same sample" drops to whatever a person is willing to vouch for from memory, which is precisely the unreliability barcoding exists to eliminate in the first place.

Barcoding and chain of custody keep a sample's identity intact as it physically moves. The next section covers where all of that identity information, along with everything else about a sample and its results, actually lives: the LIMS.
