Every liquid-handling action, however complex the protocol, breaks down into two motions: pulling liquid in and pushing it back out. Getting these two motions right — including a scoop of small details around them — is most of what separates an accurate pipetting step from a sloppy one.

### Aspirate: pulling liquid in

**Aspiration** draws liquid into the tip. Three things determine whether it goes well:

- **Tip depth.** Too shallow, and the tip pulls in air along with liquid (an **aspirate short**). Too deep, and liquid clings to the *outside* of the tip, carrying extra volume out that wasn't measured. The right depth is just below the surface, adjusted as the liquid level drops.
- **Aspiration speed.** Pulling too fast creates turbulence and air bubbles, especially in viscous liquids; pulling too slow wastes time across a large run. Speed is tuned per liquid type.
- **A settling pause.** After the piston stops moving, the liquid inside the tip needs a brief moment to stop moving too — releasing the tip immediately can leave liquid still sloshing at the exact moment it's supposed to be static.

### Dispense: pushing liquid out

**Dispensing** has its own set of decisions:

- **Contact vs. no-contact.** Dispensing with the tip touching the destination's liquid or wall (**dispense to wall** or **dispense to liquid**) controls where the droplet forms and avoids stray splashing; dispensing into open air above the well is faster but riskier for small volumes.
- **Blow-out.** Because some liquid always remains adhered to the tip's interior walls, a final extra push of air — the **blow-out** — expels that remainder. Skipping it under-delivers volume, gradually, over every single well.
- **Reverse pipetting.** For foamy or viscous liquids, drawing in *more* than the target volume and dispensing only the intended amount (leaving the excess in the tip) avoids the bubbles a standard aspirate/dispense would create.

### Why small details compound

None of these steps individually moves the needle much on a single transfer. The problem is scale: an error of half a percent per well, repeated across a 384-well plate and then across dozens of plates, becomes a systematic bias in an entire dataset — not random noise that averages out, but a consistent skew in one direction, because the same mistake (say, skipping blow-out) happens the same way every time. That's the core reason automated liquid handling exists: not because a person can't aspirate and dispense correctly once, but because a machine does the same correct sequence every single time, at any scale.
