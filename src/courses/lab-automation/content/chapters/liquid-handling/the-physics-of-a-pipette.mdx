A pipette looks simple — a plunger, a tip, a number on a dial — but underneath, it's manipulating air pressure to move liquid precisely. Understanding that mechanism explains almost every quirk and failure mode covered later in this chapter.

### Air displacement, the common case

Most pipettes work by **air displacement**: a piston moves inside a sealed cylinder, and the column of air between the piston and the liquid transmits that motion. Push the plunger down and air is expelled from the tip; the piston then rises as it draws liquid up, pulled by the resulting vacuum; push down again and that same air column pushes the liquid back out.

Because the piston never touches the liquid, the tip can be swapped between samples without contaminating the pipette's internals — that disposability is the whole reason air-displacement pipettes dominate the lab.

### Positive displacement, for the exceptions

Some liquids don't behave under an air cushion: they're too viscous, too volatile, or they foam. **Positive displacement** pipettes solve this with a piston that sits *inside* the tip itself and directly contacts the liquid, with no air gap at all. There's no air column to compress, expand, or be thrown off by a liquid's own vapor pressure, so the volume delivered matches the volume displaced far more reliably. The tradeoff is cost — the tips themselves contain a moving part, not just a disposable cone.

### Why air compresses, and why that matters

Air is compressible; liquid, for practical purposes, is not. That single fact is the root of most pipetting error. If the air column had zero compressibility, plunger travel would translate to liquid volume perfectly. In reality, some of the piston's motion goes into compressing or expanding the air rather than moving the liquid boundary — and how much depends on temperature, altitude, and the liquid's own vapor pressure pushing back into that air column. A volatile liquid (like ethanol) evaporates into the air gap and changes its own effective volume before the pipette even finishes drawing it up.

This is why pipettes are calibrated for water and why unusual liquids need different technique or hardware entirely — the physics of an air cushion doesn't treat every liquid the same, a theme the rest of this chapter builds on directly.
