Everything so far has quietly assumed a liquid that behaves like water. Most liquids don't. Understanding *why* a liquid resists standard pipetting is what lets you fix it — usually by adjusting technique rather than fighting the liquid itself.

### Viscosity: liquids that move slowly

A **viscous** liquid (glycerol, concentrated protein solutions, some polymers) resists flow. Aspirated too fast, it can't keep up with the piston, leaving the tip under-filled; dispensed too fast, it clings to the tip walls instead of releasing cleanly. The fix is almost always **speed**: slower aspiration and dispensing gives the liquid time to actually move with the air column, plus a longer settling pause before withdrawing the tip.

### Volatility: liquids that evaporate mid-transfer

A **volatile** liquid (ethanol, some organic solvents) evaporates into the air column the moment it's aspirated, building vapor pressure that pushes back against the piston — a phenomenon called **overaspiration** when it causes extra liquid to be drawn in, or dripping when that pressure pushes liquid back out after the tip is withdrawn. **Pre-wetting** — aspirating and dispensing the liquid once or twice before the "real" transfer — saturates the air column with vapor so the actual measured aspiration behaves consistently.

### Foaming and surface tension

Some liquids (detergents, some protein and cell-culture media) foam when agitated, and foam is mostly air — a tip that aspirates through foam pulls in far less true liquid than the volume suggests. Reducing aspiration speed and aspirating from below the foam layer both help; for the worst cases, reverse pipetting (drawing extra volume and dispensing only the target amount) sidesteps the problem by never needing a precise dispense of the foamy remainder.

Liquids with very high or very low **surface tension** create their own issues at the tip opening: high surface tension can cause a droplet to hang off the tip instead of releasing; low surface tension (many surfactant-containing liquids) can cause liquid to creep up the *outside* of the tip. Dispensing to touch the destination wall or liquid, rather than into open air, resolves most of this.

### Why software, not just technique, is involved

On an automated liquid handler, these fixes aren't manual habits — they're **liquid classes**: named parameter sets (aspirate speed, pre-wet cycles, settling delay, dispense mode) stored in software and selected per reagent. Defining a correct liquid class once means every future protocol using that reagent inherits the right handling automatically, instead of relying on whoever programs the run to remember that this particular buffer foams.

Even with the right technique and the right liquid class, none of this matters if the pipette itself isn't actually delivering the volume it claims to — which is exactly what the final section of this chapter checks.
