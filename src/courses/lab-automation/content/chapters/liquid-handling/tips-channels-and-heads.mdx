The physics from the first section and the technique from the second are both executed through physical hardware: the disposable tip, the channel that drives it, and the head that holds many channels together. This section is about that hardware and the choices it forces.

### Tips: disposable, and not interchangeable

A pipette tip is the only part of the system that touches liquid, which is exactly why it's disposable — swap it and there's no carryover between samples. But tips aren't one-size-fits-all:

- **Volume range.** A tip rated for 0.5–20 µL behaves differently from one rated for 200–1000 µL; using a tip far outside its designed range hurts accuracy at both extremes.
- **Filtered tips.** A small filter plug near the top blocks aerosols and liquid from reaching the pipette's internals — essential when working with volatile or infectious samples, since it protects the instrument from what it aspirates.
- **Low-retention tips.** A specially treated inner surface that liquid clings to less, reducing the residual film that blow-out has to deal with — useful for viscous or precious samples where every drop counts.

### Channels: one pipetting unit

A **channel** is a single, independent pipetting mechanism — its own piston, its own air column, capable of aspirating and dispensing on its own. A single-channel pipette has exactly one; it's flexible but slow across many wells.

### Heads: many channels, moved together

A **head** ties multiple channels into one assembly that moves and fires as a unit. The two configurations that matter most:

- **8- or 12-channel heads** pipette an entire column or row of a 96-well plate in one motion — matched to the plate's own spacing so every channel lines up with a well simultaneously.
- **96- or 384-channel heads** pipette an *entire plate* in a single stroke. Every channel is fixed relative to the others, so all wells receive the same volume in the same action — extremely fast, but inflexible: it can't easily give well A1 a different volume than well H12 the way independent channels can.

### Independent channels: a middle ground

Some liquid handlers use **independent-channel** heads — multiple channels in one assembly, but each with its own piston, able to aspirate or dispense a *different* volume from its neighbors, or skip a well entirely. This combines a head's speed with a single channel's flexibility, at the cost of a more complex (and expensive) mechanism.

### Matching hardware to the protocol

Choosing tips, channels, and heads is really choosing where a protocol sits between flexibility and speed. A method that treats every well identically — like transferring the same reagent to a full plate — is a natural fit for a fixed 96-channel head. A method that needs different volumes in different wells, or skips some wells based on a plate map, needs independent channels or single-channel flexibility instead. Picking the wrong hardware for a protocol's shape either wastes speed or, worse, forces awkward workarounds to fake flexibility the hardware doesn't have.

Not every liquid behaves the same under this hardware, though — the next section covers what happens when a liquid actively resists being pipetted at all.
