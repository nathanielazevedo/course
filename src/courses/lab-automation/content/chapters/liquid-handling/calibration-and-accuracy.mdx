Everything in this chapter — physics, technique, hardware, liquid classes — assumes the pipette is actually delivering the volume it claims to. That assumption has to be checked, regularly and rigorously, or every downstream measurement inherits a hidden error. This section is about how that check works.

### Accuracy and precision are different things

**Accuracy** is how close the delivered volume is to the *target* volume — set a pipette to 100 µL, and accuracy asks whether it actually delivered 100 µL. **Precision** is how close repeated deliveries are to *each other*, regardless of whether they hit the target. A pipette can be precise but inaccurate (consistently delivering 95 µL every time it's set to 100), or accurate on average but imprecise (bouncing between 90 and 110 µL around a true mean of 100). Both matter, but they're diagnosed and fixed differently — accuracy by adjustment, precision by mechanical wear or technique.

### How calibration is actually checked

The standard method is **gravimetric**: dispense a target volume of water onto a precision balance and weigh it. Because water's density at a known temperature is well established, weight converts directly to volume. Repeating this several times at a given volume setting yields both numbers at once — the mean tells you accuracy (how close to target), the spread tells you precision (how consistent). This is done at multiple volumes across the pipette's range, since error isn't necessarily uniform — a pipette can be dead-on at its maximum volume and biased at its minimum.

### Drift, and why it's inevitable

No pipette holds calibration forever. Seals wear, springs fatigue, and mechanical tolerances shift with use — a gradual process called **drift**. A pipette that passed calibration six months ago is not guaranteed to still be within tolerance today, which is why labs recalibrate on a **schedule** (often every 3–6 months, more often for high-use or high-stakes instruments) rather than only after something looks wrong. Waiting for a visible problem means every run since the last good calibration is already suspect.

### Automated systems don't get a pass

It's tempting to assume a robotic liquid handler, doing the exact same motion every time, can't drift the way a human hand can. It still can — the mechanical piston and seals wear down regardless of who or what is operating the plunger. Automated systems are calibrated the same gravimetric way, often with the check built directly into routine maintenance, precisely because "the machine does it the same way every time" only guards against *inconsistency* — it says nothing about whether that consistent action is the *correct* one.

Calibration closes the loop on this chapter: physics explains why volume can drift, technique and hardware choices manage that drift for difficult liquids, and calibration is how a lab actually verifies, on a schedule, that none of it has quietly gone wrong.
