Every instrument covered so far can do its one job well. What none of them can do alone is decide *when* to do it relative to everything else happening on the same deck. That coordination question — the scheduling problem — is what turns a collection of capable instruments into a single working system.

### Why one instrument at a time isn't the bottleneck

A liquid handler pipetting a plate, a reader measuring it, and a washer cleaning the next one could each, individually, run as fast as their own hardware allows. The actual constraint is almost never any single instrument's speed — it's the fact that they all share a limited number of resources: one robotic arm to move plates between them, a fixed number of nests to hold plates in transit, and often a strict order dictated by the assay's chemistry (you can't read a plate before it's washed). Scheduling exists because these shared, limited resources force decisions no individual instrument can make on its own.

### The core question a scheduler answers

At its simplest, a scheduler decides: given several plates that each need a sequence of steps done, and a shared set of instruments and transport to do them with, what runs next, on what, right now? That question sounds simple for one plate running through one sequence of steps in isolation. It becomes genuinely hard the moment multiple plates are in flight simultaneously, each at a different stage of its own sequence, all competing for the same reader, the same arm, the same tip boxes.

### Why a bad schedule looks like a slow system, not a wrong one

A poorly scheduled system doesn't usually produce wrong results — it produces a *slow* one. Plates sit waiting for an arm that's busy elsewhere; a reader idles while the next plate is still five steps away from being ready for it. Nothing is technically broken, but throughput — the very number covered back in the automated-laboratory chapter — quietly collapses, because the schedule failed to keep the shared resources continuously busy on useful work.

The remaining sections of this chapter break the scheduling problem down piece by piece: how far in advance a schedule can be planned, how shared resources are actually allocated, how timing constraints between steps are enforced, and what happens when something in a running schedule goes wrong.
