Whether a schedule is static, dynamic, or somewhere between, it's ultimately deciding who gets to use what, and when. This section is about the resources actually being contested, and the specific ways two plates can end up wanting the same thing at the same time.

### What counts as a resource

Almost anything shared across plates is a resource a scheduler has to allocate: the robotic arm (only one plate can be moved at a time), each instrument (a reader can only measure one plate at once), each deck position (a nest can only hold one plate at a time), and consumables like tip boxes and reagent reservoirs, which run out and eventually need replenishing. A scheduler's real job is tracking the state of every one of these — busy or free, full or empty — and never assigning a plate to something already claimed by another.

### The four classic conflict types

- **Contention** — two plates need the same instrument at the same moment. One must wait; the scheduler decides which, based on priority or how much delay each can tolerate.
- **Deadlock** — plate A is waiting for a resource plate B holds, while plate B is waiting for a resource plate A holds. Neither can proceed, and without detection, the whole system stalls silently rather than crashing loudly.
- **Starvation** — a low-priority plate keeps getting passed over in favor of higher-priority work, and in the worst case, never actually gets its turn at all.
- **Resource exhaustion** — a shared consumable (tips, reagent volume) runs out mid-run, and no plate needing it can proceed until it's replenished.

### Preventing conflicts versus resolving them

Some conflicts can be designed away entirely — pre-allocating a fixed nest to a plate for its whole run avoids ever contending for nest space, at the cost of holding that nest reserved even during steps that don't need it. Other conflicts are cheaper to just detect and resolve as they arise: a scheduler that notices a would-be deadlock before committing to it, and reorders which plate proceeds first, avoids the whole scenario without needing to reserve resources defensively the entire time.

### Why this matters more as scale increases

With one or two plates in flight, resource conflicts rarely materialize — there's little to contend over. As a system runs dozens of plates simultaneously to maximize throughput, contention, starvation, and exhaustion become the normal case rather than the exception, and a scheduler's ability to handle them gracefully is what actually determines whether adding more concurrent work increases throughput or just increases the number of things waiting on each other.

Resource conflicts are about *what* two steps compete over. The next section covers a related but distinct constraint: *when*, in time, a step is allowed to happen relative to the ones around it.
