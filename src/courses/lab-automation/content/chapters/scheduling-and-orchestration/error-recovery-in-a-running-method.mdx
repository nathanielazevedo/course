Every scheduling strategy in this chapter assumes things go according to plan. They don't, always. A tip fails to attach, an instrument reports an error, a plate gets stuck mid-transport — and the scheduler has to decide what happens to that one plate, and to everything else still running around it.

### The first decision: stop everything, or isolate the problem?

When something goes wrong with one plate, a scheduler has two broad options. **Halt the whole run** — the safest option when the failure might reflect something wrong with a shared resource (a jammed arm, a miscalibrated instrument) that could just as easily corrupt every other plate too. **Isolate the failure** — pull the one affected plate aside and let every other, unaffected plate continue on schedule, which preserves throughput but only makes sense if the failure is genuinely local to that one plate and nothing it touched afterward.

### Retry, skip, or escalate

Once a failure is contained to a single plate (or the whole run, if halted), the scheduler needs a concrete response, not just a pause. **Retry** — attempt the same step again, appropriate for a transient issue like a tip that failed to attach on the first try. **Skip** — mark the step as failed and continue with the rest of the plate's sequence, appropriate when a later analysis can still make sense of a plate missing one data point. **Escalate** — stop and alert a person, appropriate when the failure is outside anything the system was built to resolve on its own. Choosing the wrong response is its own kind of failure: retrying a fundamentally broken step wastes time and consumables without ever succeeding; skipping a step whose failure genuinely invalidates the whole sample quietly produces bad data downstream.

### Checkpointing: knowing where a plate actually is

None of these responses work without the scheduler knowing, precisely, what step a given plate was on and what state it was in when the failure occurred — its **checkpoint**. Without an accurate checkpoint, a retry might redo a step that actually succeeded (over-processing a sample, or wasting a reagent it didn't need again), or skip past a step that never actually ran. Robust systems record a checkpoint after every completed step specifically so a plate's exact position in its sequence is never in doubt, even mid-failure.

### Why this connects back to everything earlier in the chapter

Error recovery isn't a separate system bolted onto scheduling — it's the same resource-allocation and timing logic from the previous two sections, just triggered by an unplanned event instead of a planned one. A plate pulled aside for isolation still occupies a nest that a scheduler must now account for; a retried step still has to compete for the same instrument and arm time as everything else in flight. Handling failure gracefully is really just handling the scheduling problem correctly under conditions the original plan didn't anticipate.
