Resource conflicts are about *what* two steps compete over. Just as often, the constraint isn't a shared resource at all — it's the clock itself. Many lab protocols care not just that a step happens, but exactly how much time passes before or after it.

### Sequence dependencies: order that must be preserved

The simplest timing constraint is pure ordering: step B cannot start until step A finishes, because B's input is A's output — you can't read a plate that hasn't been washed yet, or wash a plate that hasn't finished incubating. This kind of dependency is absolute; there's no acceptable window, just a strict "not before."

### Timing windows: a minimum and a maximum

Many protocols add a stricter constraint on top of ordering: not just *that* B follows A, but *how long* after. An incubation step might specify "wait exactly 30 minutes" — too short and the reaction hasn't finished; too long and it's overdeveloped, sometimes producing a worse result than not running the step at all. Some steps specify only a **minimum** wait (at least this long, more is fine); others specify a true **window** (no earlier than X, no later than Y) — a much harder constraint for a scheduler to hit reliably, since it has to land a step within a narrow band of time rather than just avoid rushing it.

### Why timing windows are the hardest constraint to guarantee

A pure ordering constraint only requires the scheduler to avoid starting B before A finishes — trivially satisfiable by just waiting long enough. A timing window requires the scheduler to predict, in advance, exactly when a resource will be free to run B at the right moment — accounting for every other plate that might also want that same resource around the same time. This is where the scheduling problem, resource conflicts, and timing constraints all meet directly: satisfying a tight timing window for one plate can require deliberately reserving a resource in advance, even holding it idle, so it's guaranteed available at the exact moment the window demands.

### Multiple plates with overlapping windows

The real difficulty arrives when several plates each have their own timing windows, staggered across a run, all needing access to the same reader or washer at different, narrow moments. A scheduler juggling this has to plan several steps ahead rather than deciding purely reactively — which is exactly why pure dynamic scheduling struggles with tight timing constraints, and why most real systems lean more static specifically around these windows even if they stay dynamic elsewhere.

Ordering and timing constraints describe what a schedule is *supposed* to do. The final section of this chapter covers what happens when, despite all of this planning, something in a running method still goes wrong.
