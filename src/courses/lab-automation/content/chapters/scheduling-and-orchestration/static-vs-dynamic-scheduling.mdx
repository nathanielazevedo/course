Given the scheduling problem, there are two fundamentally different strategies for solving it: work everything out in advance, or decide as you go. Most real systems land somewhere between the two, but understanding the pure forms first makes the tradeoff clear.

### Static scheduling: the plan is fixed before the run starts

**Static scheduling** computes the entire timeline in advance — every instrument assignment, every timing, for every plate — before the method ever begins executing. Because the whole plan exists up front, it can be checked and optimized as a whole: a scheduling algorithm can search for the arrangement that minimizes idle time or maximizes throughput, in a way that's very hard to do if decisions are only made one step at a time. The tradeoff is fragility — a static schedule assumes the world matches the plan exactly, and it has no natural way to react to something running a few seconds slower or faster than predicted.

### Dynamic scheduling: decide as you go

**Dynamic scheduling** decides what runs next in real time, based on the system's actual current state, rather than a plan made in advance. Instead of computing every instrument assignment ahead of time, a dynamic scheduler continuously asks "what's ready to run, and what's free to run it on?" and assigns work accordingly. This handles real-world variability gracefully — an instrument running long simply delays what depends on it, without invalidating a master plan — but sacrifices the global optimization a static schedule can achieve, since decisions are made locally and can't see far into the future.

### Why real systems blend both

Pure static scheduling is brittle in a physical world where instrument timing always has some variance. Pure dynamic scheduling, decided moment to moment with no lookahead, can make locally reasonable choices that turn out badly later — assigning the arm to a low-priority task right before a time-critical one becomes ready, for instance. Most production schedulers use a **hybrid**: a static plan sets rough priorities and expected timing, while a dynamic layer handles the moment-to-moment execution and adapts when reality departs from that plan.

### The real tradeoff: predictability versus resilience

Choosing where a system sits between static and dynamic is really choosing between predictability and resilience. A highly static schedule is easy to reason about and audit before it ever runs — useful in regulated environments where a method's exact timeline matters. A more dynamic schedule tolerates the unpredictability of real hardware better, at the cost of a schedule that's harder to fully predict in advance. Neither is universally correct; the right balance depends on how much variability the actual hardware produces and how much that variability can be tolerated.
