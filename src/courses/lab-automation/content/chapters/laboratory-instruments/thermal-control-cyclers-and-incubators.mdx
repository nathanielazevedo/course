Many reactions in a lab don't happen at room temperature — they need to be held steady, or moved through a precise sequence of temperatures, before anything measurable occurs. Two instruments do most of that work, and they solve very different problems.

### Incubators: hold steady

An **incubator** maintains one condition — usually temperature, often humidity and CO₂ as well — over an extended period, from hours to days. Cell culture is the classic use: living cells need 37 °C, roughly 5% CO₂, and high humidity to survive and grow, and any of those drifting for long stretches can kill an entire plate of cells. An incubator's whole job is *stability*: hold the setpoint against whatever the outside environment (or the door opening) throws at it.

### Thermal cyclers: move through a program

A **thermal cycler** does the opposite — it deliberately and rapidly moves a sample through a repeating sequence of temperatures. The canonical use is **PCR** (polymerase chain reaction), which needs three temperature steps repeated 20–40 times: a high temperature to separate DNA strands (**denaturation**), a lower temperature for short DNA primers to bind (**annealing**), and a middle temperature for an enzyme to copy the strand (**extension**). Getting from one step to the next quickly and evenly across every well is what separates a good thermal cycler from a slow one.

### The engineering problem: even, fast temperature change

Both instrument types face the same underlying challenge: a plate is a large, uneven mass, and heat doesn't move through it instantly. A thermal cycler's block must bring every well to the *same* temperature at the *same* time — a well at the plate's edge that lags behind the center by even a degree or two can mean that well's reaction under- or over-performs relative to its neighbors, silently. This is why cyclers use metal blocks with active heating and cooling elements (Peltier devices, most commonly) rather than an air oven — metal conducts heat far more evenly and quickly than air can.

Incubators face the inverse version of the same problem: instead of moving fast, they must resist disturbance. Opening the door to load a plate lets in room-temperature, low-humidity air, and a well-designed incubator recovers its setpoint quickly rather than leaving cells in a swinging environment for the next hour.

### Automating around them

Both instruments are naturally suited to automation because their "action" is just time passing at a controlled condition — a scheduler hands a plate to an incubator or cycler, waits the required duration, and then retrieves it, with the instrument itself doing nothing that needs supervision in between. That waiting period, multiplied across many plates running staggered programs, is exactly the kind of scheduling problem covered later in this course.

Temperature is one axis of instrument control; the next section covers instruments that move plates and samples through mechanical, rather than thermal, forces.
