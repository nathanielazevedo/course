Not every instrument in a workflow reads a signal or holds a temperature. Some just apply force — spinning a plate, sealing it shut, or opening it back up — and these mechanical steps are just as essential, and just as prone to going wrong, as anything more sophisticated.

### Centrifuges: separation by spinning

A **centrifuge** spins labware fast enough that centrifugal force separates components by density — pelleting cells to the bottom of a tube, driving liquid out of a filter plate, or removing bubbles from a viscous dispense. The key parameter is **relative centrifugal force (RCF)**, measured in multiples of gravity (× g), not raw rotational speed — the same RPM produces different RCF depending on the rotor's radius, so protocols specify RCF to stay consistent across different centrifuge models.

Centrifuges have a requirement most instruments don't: **balance**. Spinning an asymmetric load — one plate loaded, the opposite position empty — creates a vibration that can damage the rotor or, in a bad case, the instrument itself. Automated systems must load counterweighted plates or matched pairs into opposing positions, a real scheduling constraint covered in a later chapter.

### Sealers: closing a plate

A **sealer** applies a film or foil layer across the top of a plate, closing every well at once — protecting samples from evaporation, contamination, or spillage during a spin, a shake, or an overnight incubation. Heat sealers bond an adhesive foil using heat and pressure for a strong, long-term seal (common before shipping or extended storage); pierceable seals use a lighter film that a pipette tip can push through directly, letting later steps access the wells without a separate peeling step.

### Peelers: opening it back up

A **peeler** does the reverse: removing a seal cleanly so the plate can be accessed again. This sounds trivial but isn't — pulling too fast can splash or aerosolize well contents, and an incompletely removed seal can leave shreds of foil that show up as debris in the very wells about to be measured.

### Why these steps sit at the boundaries of a workflow

Centrifuging, sealing, and peeling almost always happen at the *transitions* of a workflow — before or after a step that can't tolerate an open, unsealed, or unbalanced plate. A protocol that seals a plate before an overnight incubation and peels it again the next morning is really using the seal as a form of protection during a period when no one is watching. Getting these transition steps wrong doesn't corrupt the science directly the way a bad pipetting step would — it corrupts the *conditions* the science depends on, which can be just as damaging and considerably harder to trace back to its source.

Mechanical steps like these prepare a plate for measurement or storage; the next section looks at the instruments that handle a related mechanical task — washing plates and dispensing liquid in bulk.
