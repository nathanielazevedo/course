Many assays — an ELISA especially — depend on removing everything unbound from a well while leaving the bound target untouched, then refilling that well with the next reagent. Doing this well, and doing it fast across a whole plate, is the job of two closely related instruments.

### Plate washers: aspirate, then dispense, many times over

A **plate washer** repeats a simple cycle across every well: aspirate out the current liquid, dispense in a wash buffer, and repeat several times to flush away anything not specifically bound to the well's surface — antibodies, unreacted dye, cellular debris. The core engineering challenge is the aspiration step: a probe that sits too high leaves residual liquid behind (diluting or contaminating the next reagent); a probe that sits too low can scrape or damage the well's coated surface, destroying the very binding the assay depends on. Wash **residual volume** — how much liquid is left behind after the final aspirate — is a number vendors specify and labs verify, because a "clean" well that still holds a few microliters of the wrong liquid isn't actually clean.

### Bulk dispensers: fast, identical fills

A **bulk dispenser** exists for the opposite half of the same problem: filling every well of a plate with the *same* liquid, fast. Rather than a pipetting head aspirating and dispensing well by well, most bulk dispensers push liquid through a manifold of fixed nozzles — one per column or one per well — so an entire plate fills in one motion. This only works because the liquid is identical everywhere; a bulk dispenser has no way to give different wells different volumes or different reagents, trading that flexibility for raw speed on the one task it's built for.

### Cross-contamination risk in a shared manifold

Because a washer's probes and a dispenser's manifold touch every well in sequence (or simultaneously, through shared plumbing), any residue left in the tubing carries into the next plate. Both instrument types require a **prime** and **flush** cycle — running buffer through the lines before and after use — to clear out air bubbles and leftover liquid from a previous reagent. Skipping this step doesn't fail loudly; it just adds a small, consistent contamination to every well of the next run, the same category of silent, systematic error covered earlier in this course.

Washers and dispensers round out the instruments that manipulate plate contents directly. The final section of this chapter turns to instruments built for a very different purpose: seeing, rather than measuring, what's inside a well.
