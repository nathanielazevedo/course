A liquid handler moves samples into place; a plate reader is usually what finally asks "what's the answer?" It shines something at a plate, or waits for something to come out of it, and turns that into a number. Which physical phenomenon it measures determines what kind of assay it can read.

### Absorbance: how much light gets through

An **absorbance** reader shines light of a specific wavelength through a well and measures how much comes out the other side. A colored product — from an enzyme reaction, say — blocks more light than a colorless well, and the amount blocked follows a predictable relationship with concentration. This is the oldest and simplest detection mode, and the one most assays (like a basic ELISA) are built around.

### Fluorescence: light in, different light out

A **fluorescence** reader excites a sample with light at one wavelength and measures light re-emitted at a longer wavelength. Because it measures emitted light against a dark background rather than a small dip in transmitted light, fluorescence is far more sensitive than absorbance — useful when a signal is faint. The tradeoff is more finicky physics: excitation and emission wavelengths must be tuned to the specific fluorescent dye or protein in use, and background fluorescence from plastic or media can add noise.

### Luminescence: no light in at all

A **luminescence** reader doesn't shine anything on the sample — it measures light the sample generates itself, usually from a chemical reaction (like the luciferase reaction used in many cell-viability assays). With no excitation light to filter out, luminescence has essentially no background signal, making it extremely sensitive, though the signal is often transient and must be read within a specific window after the reaction starts.

### Reading top or bottom

A reader can approach a plate from above (**top read**) or below (**bottom read**). Bottom reads are common for cell-based assays in clear-bottom plates, since cells settle on the bottom and reading through the plastic avoids disturbing them; top reads are typical for solution-based absorbance assays and are the only option for opaque-bottom plates.

### Why the choice of mode is decided by the assay, not the reader

A single high-end reader often supports all three modes, but which one a *given* assay uses is fixed by its chemistry — an ELISA that produces a colored product needs absorbance; a cell-viability assay built on a luciferase reaction needs luminescence. Choosing the reader mode isn't a preference, it's dictated upstream by which reaction the protocol was designed around, which is why the reader's driver (covered in a later chapter) needs to expose all three modes rather than assuming one.
