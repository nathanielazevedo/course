Labware doesn't move itself between instruments — something has to physically pick it up, carry it, and set it down precisely. In many automated labs, that something is a robotic arm, and understanding how an arm actually moves starts with counting how many independent ways it can move at all.

### Degrees of freedom

A **degree of freedom (DOF)** is one independent way a robot's end can move or rotate — one joint, one axis. A simple arm that only slides along a single rail has one degree of freedom: it can move back and forth, and nothing else. An arm that can also lift up and down adds a second. Real lab robots typically have between three and six degrees of freedom, and the number determines what positions and orientations the arm's gripper can actually reach.

### Cartesian arms: simple and predictable

A **Cartesian (gantry) arm** moves along three straight, perpendicular axes — X, Y, and Z — much like a 3D printer's print head. Each axis moves independently in a straight line, which makes the arm's motion easy to predict and its mechanics comparatively simple and precise. The tradeoff is reach and flexibility: a Cartesian arm is generally confined to the rectangular volume its rails cover, and it approaches everything from directly above.

### Articulated arms: more freedom, more complexity

An **articulated (jointed) arm** — the kind that looks like a human arm, with a shoulder, elbow, and wrist — uses rotating joints instead of straight rails, typically giving it four to six degrees of freedom. This lets it reach around obstacles, approach a plate from an angle, and access positions a Cartesian arm's straight-line axes simply can't get to. The cost is complexity: more joints means more places for small errors to accumulate, and calculating exactly how each joint must rotate to reach a target position is a harder computation than moving three independent straight axes.

### Matching the arm to the deck

The right choice of arm depends on the deck it serves. A compact system with instruments arranged in a simple line or grid, all approached from above, is well served by a Cartesian arm's straightforward, predictable motion. A larger, more crowded deck — instruments at different heights, some tucked behind others — often needs an articulated arm's ability to route around obstacles that a straight-line gantry simply couldn't clear.

Degrees of freedom describe how an arm's *joints* can move; what actually happens at the very end of that arm — how it grips a plate once it gets there — is the subject of the next section.
