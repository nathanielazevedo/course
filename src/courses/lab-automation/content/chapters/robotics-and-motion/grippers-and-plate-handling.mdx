Degrees of freedom get an arm's end to the right place. What's attached at that end — the gripper — is what actually takes hold of a plate, and getting that grip wrong is one of the most common ways an automated run fails physically rather than chemically.

### The basic gripper: two fingers and a squeeze

Most lab grippers are a **parallel-jaw gripper**: two fingers that close toward each other, squeezing a plate from opposite sides. Because plates share the SBS footprint, a gripper tuned to the standard plate width can grab any plate on the deck the same way, regardless of well count or well density inside it — the same reasoning that made the SBS standard matter for instrument nests applies just as directly to a gripper's jaws.

### How hard is hard enough?

Grip force is a genuine balancing act. Too loose, and the plate can shift or slip mid-transport — dropped, or worse, tilted enough to spill its contents without falling outright. Too tight, and rigid plastic plates can flex or crack, particularly thin-walled ones, and a plate held slightly out of true can seat incorrectly in the next nest it's placed into. Grippers are tuned, and in more advanced systems actively force-controlled, to apply just enough grip to prevent slipping without over-compressing the plate.

### Alternatives to squeezing

Not every gripper works by squeezing. **Vacuum grippers** use suction cups to lift a plate (or a lid) from above by adhering to a flat surface rather than clamping its sides — useful for thin lids or delicate labware that a jaw-style grip could damage. **Underside support grippers** slide a flat platform beneath the plate's flange rather than gripping its sides at all, which avoids any squeeze-related risk entirely at the cost of needing clear space to slide the platform in.

### Handling more than just plates

A gripper's job often extends beyond moving a closed plate: removing and replacing a plate's lid, separating a stack of plates one at a time from a hotel, or placing a plate precisely enough into a nest that has almost no tolerance for being off by even a millimeter. Each of these is a slightly different physical problem, and systems that need to do all of them sometimes swap grippers mid-run, or use a single gripper engineered with enough range to handle several tasks adequately rather than any one of them perfectly.

Grippers get a plate reliably into and out of an arm's grasp. Where the arm actually knows to move it *to*, precisely and repeatably, is a question of coordinate systems — the subject of the next section.
