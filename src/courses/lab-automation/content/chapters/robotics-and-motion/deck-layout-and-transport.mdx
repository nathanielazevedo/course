Individual taught positions are the atoms; the deck layout is how those atoms are arranged into a coherent whole, and transport is how labware actually flows between them once a method starts running.

### The deck as a floor plan

A **deck** is the physical work surface an automated system is built around — a grid of fixed positions where instruments, plate nests, tip boxes, and reservoirs sit. Laying out a deck is a genuinely physical design problem: instruments an arm needs to reach must be within its actual working envelope, positions used together frequently should sit close to minimize travel time, and anything requiring a person's occasional intervention (a reagent refill, a tip-box swap) needs to remain physically accessible without interrupting the rest of the deck.

### Transport: the moves between positions

**Transport** is the actual act of an arm (or conveyor, or track) carrying a piece of labware from one deck position to another. Every transport move is really three sub-steps: pick up from the source position, travel a path to the destination, and place down at the destination position — each of which the taught coordinates and gripper technique from earlier sections make possible.

### Paths, not just points

Moving from point A to point B isn't simply a straight line drawn between two taught coordinates. An arm's **path** — the exact route and height it travels — has to clear every obstacle between source and destination: an instrument's housing, another plate already in transit, the arm's own moving parts on a multi-arm system. Paths are often taught or programmed with specific waypoints and safe travel heights, ensuring the arm always lifts clear of obstacles before moving horizontally, rather than assuming a direct line is ever safe.

### When multiple arms or transports share a deck

Larger systems sometimes use more than one arm, or a mix of arms and conveyors, splitting up transport duties across a bigger deck than one arm could reach alone. This introduces genuine coordination requirements — two arms must never be scheduled to occupy the same physical space at the same time, a constraint that connects directly to the collision-avoidance concerns of the next section and the scheduling conflicts covered later in this course.

Deck layout and transport describe *how* labware physically moves; making sure that movement never collides with anything else moving at the same time is the final, and arguably most safety-critical, concern of this chapter.
