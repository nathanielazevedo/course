A robotic arm doesn't know a person's hand is in its path any more than it knows a plate is fragile — it only knows the coordinates and paths it was given. Safety and collision avoidance are what stand between "the arm does exactly what it was programmed to do" and "the arm does that safely, even when something unplanned is in the way."

### Collisions between moving parts

The most direct risk is mechanical: an arm's own path intersecting something it shouldn't — another arm, an instrument's door mid-close, a plate left in the wrong spot by a prior step that went slightly wrong. Systems guard against this with **soft limits** (software-defined boundaries the arm is never allowed to command itself past, even if physically capable of reaching further) and, on multi-arm systems, **zone locking** — a shared area that only one arm may occupy at a time, with the other required to wait until it's clear, directly extending the coordination problem from the previous section.

### Sensing the unexpected

Soft limits and zone locks only guard against *planned* collisions the system already knows to avoid. Genuinely unexpected obstacles — a dropped tip box, a person reaching in — need active sensing. **Light curtains** (a plane of infrared beams around the working area) and **area scanners** detect an intrusion and can halt motion immediately; some arms carry **force/torque sensing** in the joints themselves, stopping the moment resistance exceeds what a normal, unobstructed motion should ever encounter.

### Two different failure philosophies

Preventing planned collisions (soft limits, zone locking) and detecting unplanned ones (light curtains, force sensing) solve different problems. The first assumes the system's own model of the deck is correct and simply won't let the arm violate it. The second assumes that model can be wrong, or that something outside it — a person, a dropped object — has entered a space the system never planned for. A safe system needs both: perfect adherence to a flawed plan is not safety, and neither is sensing alone without any planned boundaries to begin with.

### Fail-safe, not just fail-stop

A well-designed safety system doesn't just stop on detecting a problem — it stops in a state that doesn't make things worse. An arm gripping a plate that suddenly halts mid-motion should hold its grip rather than release and drop the plate; an emergency stop should de-energize motors in a way that leaves the arm exactly where it was, not wherever momentum carries it. This distinction — failing safely, not just failing — is why safety systems are engineered and certified separately from the productivity features of the same robot, and why they're treated as non-negotiable rather than tunable for convenience.
