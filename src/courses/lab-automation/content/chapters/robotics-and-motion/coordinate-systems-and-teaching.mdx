An arm with the right degrees of freedom and a gripper tuned to hold a plate still doesn't know where anything on the deck actually is. Every position it visits — a plate nest, a tip box, an instrument's loading slot — has to be defined precisely, once, before the arm can find it reliably ever again.

### A shared coordinate system

A deck's layout is described using a **coordinate system** — a set of X, Y, and Z values measured from one fixed reference point, usually a corner of the deck itself. Every position the arm needs to reach — where plate nest 3 sits, where the reader's loading slot is, how high the arm must lift a plate to clear a wall — is stored as a coordinate in that same shared system. This is what lets a scheduler tell the arm "move to nest 3" without needing to know or care what nest 3's coordinates actually are; it just looks them up.

### Teaching: recording where things really are

In theory, every position could be calculated from the deck's engineering drawings. In practice, small physical variations — a nest mounted slightly off-spec, an instrument installed a few millimeters from its nominal position — mean the *real* deck rarely matches the drawing exactly. **Teaching** is the process of manually moving the arm to each real position once, using a control pendant or software jog controls, and recording the exact coordinates the arm found there. Every position an arm will ever need to visit is taught this way before the system runs its first real protocol.

### Named positions, not raw numbers

Once taught, positions are almost always referred to by a **name** — "plate nest 3," "reader input slot" — rather than by their raw coordinates, in the same way a method refers to "the reader" rather than the reader's specific command syntax. This is deliberate: if a nest is physically relocated, only its recorded coordinate needs updating, and every method that refers to it by name keeps working without being rewritten, the same separation-of-concerns idea that made the anatomy of an automated system maintainable in the first place.

### Why re-teaching is unavoidable after physical change

Because taught positions capture the deck's *real*, physical layout rather than its nominal design, any physical change — moving an instrument, replacing a nest, even a hard bump that shifts something slightly — invalidates the taught position for whatever moved. Systems don't automatically detect this; a position that's been physically disturbed but not re-taught will send the arm confidently to the *wrong* spot, which is why re-teaching after any hardware change is a required step, not an optional precaution.
