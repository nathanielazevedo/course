An experiment is built from a small vocabulary of physical actions. Almost every protocol — and almost every automated method — is some sequence of the same handful of moves. Learn these and you can read most protocols, because a protocol is just these verbs applied to the vessels and liquids from the previous sections.

### Pipetting

**Pipetting** is moving a measured volume of liquid from one place to another. It is *the* fundamental lab action, and the one automation is built around. A pipette works by displacing air: a plunger draws liquid up into a disposable **tip** (**aspirate**), then pushes it back out (**dispense**).

Two patterns cover most pipetting:

- **Transfer** — aspirate from a source, dispense the whole amount into a destination. One-to-one.
- **Mixing** — aspirate and dispense repeatedly in the *same* well to blend its contents into a uniform solution. ("Pipette up and down to mix.")

A **multi-channel** pipette has many tips on one head (8, 12, 96, or more), so it pipettes a whole row, column, or plate in a single motion — this parallelism is most of where a liquid handler's speed comes from. Accuracy depends on the liquid: water-like solutions pipette cleanly, while thick (**viscous**) or foamy liquids fight back, a problem covered in Chapter 02.

### Centrifugation

A **centrifuge** spins tubes or plates at high speed. The spin creates an effective force many times gravity that pushes denser material outward — to the *bottom* of the vessel. This is used to:

- **Pellet** solids: spin a cell suspension and the cells collect as a tight pellet at the tube's tip, leaving clear liquid (the **supernatant**) above. You can then pour or pipette off the supernatant and keep the pellet, or vice versa.
- **Separate** layers of different density, such as separating blood into plasma and cells.

Spin speed is given as **rcf** (relative centrifugal force, "× g") or as **rpm** (revolutions per minute); protocols usually specify a force and a time, e.g. "spin at 300 × g for 5 minutes."

### Incubation

**Incubation** is simply *waiting under controlled conditions* — holding samples at a set temperature (and sometimes humidity or gas) for a set time so a reaction can proceed or cells can grow. A reagent might need 30 minutes at room temperature to bind; cells might need days at 37 °C. The action is "wait," but the *conditions* and *timing* are part of the protocol and must be respected exactly.

### Washing

**Washing** removes unbound or unwanted material from a vessel without disturbing what's stuck to it. The cycle is: add wash buffer, then remove it (**aspirate** it off), repeating several times. In a plate assay, washing is how you rinse away everything that didn't bind to the well surface, leaving only the specific signal behind. Sloppy washing is a leading cause of high background and noisy results.

### Sealing and storage

Between steps, plates are often **sealed** — a film or foil is applied over the wells — to prevent **evaporation** (small volumes dry out fast) and contamination, and to allow safe transport or freezing. Samples are then held in storage at a defined temperature: room temperature, refrigerated (4 °C), or frozen (−20 °C or −80 °C) depending on how long and how stable they must stay.
