Almost every protocol is a list of volumes and concentrations. If you're comfortable with the units and the one equation that relates them, most of the lab's arithmetic stops being mysterious. This section is the math you'll reach for constantly.

### Units of volume

Lab volumes are small, so the working units are below the liter:

- **1 liter (L)** = 1,000 **milliliters (mL)**
- **1 mL** = 1,000 **microliters (µL)**

So 1 L = 1,000,000 µL. The microliter is the everyday unit of automated liquid handling — a single drop is roughly 50 µL, and a 384-well plate often holds only 20–50 µL per well. Pipettes routinely move volumes as small as 1 µL, where being off by a fraction of a microliter is a real error.

### Units of concentration

**Concentration** is amount of substance per unit of volume. Two families show up constantly:

- **Mass/volume** — e.g. **mg/mL** (milligrams per milliliter). Common for proteins and antibodies, where you care about how many grams are present.
- **Molar (M)** — **moles per liter**, the chemist's count-based unit. One **molar** is one mole of molecules per liter; you'll far more often see **millimolar (mM)** and **micromolar (µM)**. Molarity counts *molecules*, which matters when a reaction depends on how many molecules meet, not their total weight.

You don't need to convert between mass and molar units to follow a protocol — just recognize which kind a number is, and never mix them up.

### The one equation: C₁V₁ = C₂V₂

The single most-used calculation in the lab is **dilution** — making a concentrated solution weaker by adding more liquid. It's governed by one relationship:

The logic is simple: when you dilute, you add solvent but not more of the substance, so the *total amount* of substance is unchanged — it's just spread through a larger volume. C×V (concentration times volume) **is** that total amount, so C₁V₁ = C₂V₂.

**Worked example.** You have a 10 mg/mL antibody stock and need 1 mL at a working concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. How much stock do you take?

Solve for V₁: `V₁ = C₂V₂ / C₁ = (0.5 mg/mL × 1 mL) / 10 mg/mL = 0.05 mL = 50 µL`.

So you pipette **50 µL of stock** and add **950 µL of buffer** (the *diluent*) to reach 1 mL total. Note the two numbers always sum to the final volume: 50 + 950 = 1000 µL.

### Fold dilutions and dilution series

Protocols often describe dilutions as a **fold**: a "10-fold" (or "1:10") dilution means the final concentration is one-tenth of the start — 1 part stock to 9 parts diluent, for 10 parts total. (Watch the wording: "1:10" almost always means *1 in 10 total*, not 1 plus 10.)

A **serial dilution** chains these together: dilute the stock 10-fold, then dilute *that* 10-fold, and so on. Each step multiplies the dilution, so a few steps span an enormous range:

Serial dilutions are everywhere: building the **standard curve** that turns a raw reading into a real concentration, testing a drug across a range of doses, finding how far a sample can be diluted and still detected. Because each step feeds the next, an error early in the series propagates to every tube after it — which is exactly the kind of repetitive, error-prone, precision task automation is built to do better than a tired human.
