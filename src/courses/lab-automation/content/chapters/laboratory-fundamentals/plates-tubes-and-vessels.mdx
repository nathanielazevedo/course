Before anything moves on its own, you need to know what the liquid lives in. Every sample, reagent, and buffer in a lab sits inside a **vessel** — a container chosen for how much it holds, how it tolerates heat and spinning, and how easily a machine or a person can get liquid in and out. Almost all of automation is just moving liquid between vessels in the right order.

### The well plate

The workhorse of the modern lab is the **microplate**: a flat rectangular tray of small cups called **wells**, arranged in a grid. One plate holds many samples at once, which is exactly what makes parallel, high-throughput work possible.

Critically, the *outer dimensions* of the plate are the same regardless of well count — about the size of a deck of playing cards laid flat. That shared footprint is the **SBS standard**, and it is why a 96-well and a 384-well plate fit the same robot gripper, the same plate reader, and the same deck position. Chapter 04 covers that standard in depth; for now, just hold onto the idea that plates are interchangeable on the outside even when they differ on the inside.

Wells are addressed like a spreadsheet: **rows are letters (A–H), columns are numbers (1–12)**, so the top-left well is `A1` and the bottom-right of a 96-well plate is `H12`. Every instrument and every protocol refers to wells this way.

### Tubes

A **tube** holds a single sample rather than a grid of them. The everyday tube is the **microcentrifuge tube** (often called an "Eppendorf" after the brand), a small cone-bottomed plastic tube holding roughly 0.5–2 mL with a snap cap. Larger **conical tubes** (15 mL and 50 mL, the "Falcon" tubes) hold bigger volumes and stand in racks.

Tubes are used when samples are handled individually, need to be stored or frozen separately, or have to be spun in a centrifuge. Their cone bottom is deliberate: when you spin them, solids collect in a tight **pellet** at the tip.

### Reservoirs and troughs

When a machine needs to dispense the *same* liquid into many wells — a buffer, a wash solution, a shared reagent — it draws from a **reservoir** (also called a trough): an open boat-shaped container holding tens of milliliters. A multi-channel pipette dips all its tips into the reservoir at once and distributes the liquid across a plate.

### Choosing a vessel

The vessel is a design decision, not an afterthought. A few properties drive the choice:

- **Volume** — wells hold microliters, tubes and reservoirs hold milliliters. You pick the vessel that comfortably fits the volume without overfilling or leaving an unworkably tiny amount.
- **Material and optics** — plates for fluorescence or absorbance reading are made of specific plastics; a **clear** bottom lets light through for reading, while opaque **black** or **white** plates reduce optical cross-talk between wells.
- **Shape** — flat-bottom wells are good for reading, round- or V-bottom wells help collect small pellets or recover the last drop.
- **Treatment** — some plates have surfaces chemically treated so cells stick and grow, or so proteins bind (as in the ELISA plates you'll meet later).

The vessel determines what you can do with what's inside it. The next section turns to what's actually *in* these containers.
