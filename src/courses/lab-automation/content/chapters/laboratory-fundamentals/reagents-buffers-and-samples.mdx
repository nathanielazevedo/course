A vessel is only interesting because of what's in it. The liquids in a lab fall into a few roles, and knowing which role a liquid plays tells you how carefully it must be handled, whether it can be shared across wells, and what happens if it's wrong.

### The four roles

**Sample** — the thing you actually want to measure: a patient's blood, a cell extract, a purified protein, a drug candidate. The sample is usually precious and irreplaceable, often available in tiny amounts. You treat it the most carefully because you generally cannot make more of it.

**Reagent** — an "active" substance that reacts with or detects the sample and produces something you can measure. An antibody that binds a target, an enzyme that catalyzes a color change, a dye that fluoresces. Reagents are the chemistry of the experiment.

**Buffer** — a liquid whose job is to hold conditions *steady*. Most biological molecules only behave correctly within a narrow range of acidity (**pH**) and saltiness. A buffer resists changes in pH so the sample and reagents keep working as intended. Buffers also do humbler jobs: diluting, washing, and carrying other components. The most common is **PBS** (phosphate-buffered saline), a salt solution near the pH of blood.

**Control** — a sample with a *known* answer, run alongside the unknowns. A **positive control** should give a strong signal; a **negative control** (often just buffer, no sample) should give none. Controls tell you whether the experiment itself worked. If your negative control lights up, something is contaminating the system, and your real results can't be trusted.

### Concentration, the property that matters most

Every reagent and buffer has a **concentration** — how much active substance is dissolved in a given volume. Concentration, not volume, is usually what determines whether an experiment works. Add too little reagent and you get no signal; add too much and you swamp the result or waste expensive material. The next section is devoted to the math of concentration and dilution because it underlies nearly everything.

A related idea is the **stock** versus the **working** solution. Reagents are often stored **concentrated** (a "10× stock") to save space and stay stable, then **diluted** down to the **working concentration** just before use. A surprising fraction of lab work, manual or automated, is simply diluting stocks to working strength.

### Why handling differs by role

The role of a liquid changes how a machine should treat it:

- **Samples** can't be shared or pooled — each must keep its own identity, so they're pipetted one at a time with a fresh tip to avoid **cross-contamination** (carrying one sample into another).
- **Buffers and wash solutions** are identical everywhere, so they're poured into a shared reservoir and dispensed in bulk.
- **Reagents** sit in between: shared across wells, but often expensive and sometimes fragile, so volumes are kept tight and some need to stay cold.

Many reagents are also **sensitive** — they degrade in light, warmth, or air, or they're living cells that must be kept at a specific temperature. Handling rules like "keep on ice" or "protect from light" aren't fussiness; they're the difference between a working assay and a failed run. Automation has to respect these constraints just as a careful scientist would.
