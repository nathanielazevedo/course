The SBS footprint fixes a plate's outer shape, but that leaves an enormous amount still to choose: what container to use at all, and which of its many variants fits a given step of a protocol. This section covers the three basic vessel families a lab actually reaches for.

### Plates: the default for many samples at once

A **plate** is the workhorse of automated work — a fixed grid of wells (96, 384, or 1536 being the common counts) sharing one SBS footprint. Choosing among plates of the same well count still means choosing:

- **Well shape** — round wells mix and pipette more predictably; square wells pack more usable volume into the same footprint, which matters at high density.
- **Well volume and depth** — a "standard" 96-well plate holds up to roughly 300 µL; a "deep-well" 96-well plate, taller but the same footprint, holds several milliliters — used for sample prep steps that need more liquid than the assay itself.
- **Material and optical properties** — clear polystyrene for visible colorimetric assays; black plates to block stray light between wells in fluorescence; white plates to reflect and maximize signal in luminescence.
- **Surface treatment** — tissue-culture treated for cells to adhere to; untreated for simple liquid storage; specially coated for assays needing a target to bind directly to the well.

### Tubes: for individual samples

A **tube** holds a single sample independently rather than as part of a shared grid — a format for anything precious, irreplaceable, or handled one at a time before it ever meets a plate. Individual tubes (1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes being the classic example) don't share the SBS footprint at all; but **tube racks** built to the SBS footprint hold many individual tubes in SBS-compatible positions, letting a liquid handler treat a rack of 96 tubes almost like a 96-well plate for the purposes of automated pipetting.

### Reservoirs: bulk liquid, no sample identity

A **reservoir** is a single large trough, sometimes divided into a few channels, that holds one shared liquid — a wash buffer, a common reagent — for a multichannel pipette or bulk dispenser to draw from repeatedly. Because a reservoir holds no distinct samples, there's no cross-contamination risk in reusing the same trough across an entire run; its only job is supplying one liquid, in bulk, to wherever it's needed.

### Choosing the right vessel is choosing what identity means

The real decision behind plates, tubes, and reservoirs is about **identity**: does this liquid need to be tracked and kept separate (a sample — plate wells or individual tubes), or is it interchangeable with itself everywhere it appears (a shared reagent — a reservoir)? Getting this choice wrong in either direction causes real problems: pooling distinct samples into a shared reservoir destroys their individual identity permanently; treating an identical bulk reagent as if it needed per-well tracking wastes time and tips for no benefit.
