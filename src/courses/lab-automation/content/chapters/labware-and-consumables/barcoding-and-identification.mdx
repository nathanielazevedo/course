A tip is anonymous — any tip of the right type is as good as any other. A plate holding real samples is the opposite: mixing up *which* plate is which, even for a moment, can invalidate an entire run's worth of data. Barcoding is how a system tells one specific piece of labware apart from every other one that looks identical.

### Why identical-looking plates need identification at all

Two 96-well plates, side by side, are visually indistinguishable — same footprint, same well grid, same material. But one might hold patient samples and the other a set of calibration standards. A person can lose track of which is which after a distraction; a robotic system has no innate sense of "which plate is this" at all unless something on the plate itself declares it.

### Barcode types

- **1D (linear) barcodes** — a series of parallel bars encoding a short string, read by a laser or camera scanner. Simple, cheap, and long-established, but limited in how much data they can hold and sensitive to being scanned at the right angle.
- **2D barcodes (Data Matrix, QR-style)** — a grid of small squares encoding more data in less space, readable from a wider range of angles and even partially damaged. Common on tube caps and small labware where a 1D barcode wouldn't fit.
- **RFID tags** — a small chip and antenna that responds to a radio signal without needing a direct line of sight. More expensive per unit, but readable through some materials and without precise camera alignment, which matters for labware sitting inside an enclosed instrument.

### What the barcode actually holds

Almost universally, the barcode itself holds only a short identifier — not the sample data directly. The scanner reads that ID, and software looks it up in a database (the LIMS, covered in a later chapter) to retrieve everything actually known about that plate: what samples it holds, what protocol it's part of, what's happened to it so far. This separation matters because it means the physical barcode never needs updating as new information accumulates — only the database record behind it changes.

### Verification as a running habit, not a one-time check

A well-designed automated method scans a plate's barcode at nearly every step it touches the plate, not just once at the start, and compares that ID against what the method *expects* to see at that point in the run. A scan that returns the wrong ID — because a person swapped a plate's physical position, or two plates were mixed up upstream — stops the run before the mistake compounds, rather than silently continuing to process the wrong sample under the wrong plate's assumed identity.

Barcoding gives labware identity that survives being physically moved around a deck. The final section of this chapter turns to a different concern about that same labware: keeping its physical *contents* stable while it isn't being actively worked on.
