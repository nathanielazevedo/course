Every instrument covered in the last chapter — readers, cyclers, washers, imagers — has to physically accept the same plates. That's only possible because almost every plate in the world, regardless of manufacturer, shares one geometric standard. This section is about that standard and why it matters more than almost any single technique.

### One footprint, many well counts

The **SBS/ANSI standard** (originally set by the Society for Biomolecular Screening, now maintained by ANSI/SLAS) fixes the outer footprint of a microplate — its length, width, and the position of its corner notch — to the same dimensions regardless of how many wells are inside it. A 96-well plate, a 384-well plate, and a 1536-well plate all share the exact same footprint; what changes between them is only the well grid packed into that fixed rectangle.

### Why the footprint is the load-bearing detail

An instrument's plate nest, gripper, or deck slot is machined to one specific footprint. Because that footprint never changes, the same nest that holds a 96-well plate also holds a 384-well plate without adjustment — a robotic arm that's been taught to grip "a plate" doesn't need to be re-taught for every well density it might encounter. This is the entire reason interchangeability across vendors and instrument generations is possible at all: a plate reader built by one company and a liquid handler built by another can share the same plate without either one knowing anything about the other.

### What the standard does *not* fix

The standard governs the outer envelope only. Well shape (round, square, conical), well depth, well volume, material (polystyrene, polypropylene), optical properties (clear, black, white), and surface treatment (untreated, tissue-culture treated, protein-binding) are all free to vary between plates of the same footprint and well count. Choosing among these is an assay decision, covered in the next section — the standard's job is only to guarantee that whatever plate gets chosen still fits every instrument on the deck.

### A standard older than the automation built on it

The SBS footprint predates most of the automation this course covers; it exists because early screening labs needed *any* fixed reference so equipment from different vendors could interoperate at all. Every later chapter on robotics, transport, and deck layout leans on this one fact without restating it: whatever the plate's contents or well count, its outer shape is a known, fixed quantity a system can be built around once and trust forever.
