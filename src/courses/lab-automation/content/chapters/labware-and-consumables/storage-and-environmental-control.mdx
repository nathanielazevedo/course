Labware doesn't only exist while it's actively being pipetted, spun, or read — most of its life is spent waiting: on a deck between steps, in a queue overnight, or in long-term storage between runs entirely. What happens to it during that waiting time can matter just as much as what happens during active processing.

### What degrades while nothing seems to be happening

Samples and reagents left at the wrong temperature, humidity, or light exposure degrade even when no instrument is actively touching them. A protein sample left at room temperature for hours can denature; a light-sensitive reagent left under lab lighting can break down before it's ever used; an open plate left in dry air can lose volume to evaporation well by well, unevenly, well before anyone would think of it as an "error" in the assay itself. None of these failures look like a broken instrument — they look like an assay that simply didn't work, and tracing them back to a storage lapse is often much harder than tracing a mechanical failure.

### On-deck storage: short-term holding within a run

Many automated systems include an **on-deck hotel** or **carousel** — a small refrigerated or ambient storage unit built into the system itself, holding plates that are queued for a later step in the same run. This solves a real timing problem: a scheduler often needs a plate to sit for an exact incubation period between two steps, and an on-deck storage unit lets that waiting happen under controlled conditions without occupying a full external incubator or requiring a person to intervene.

### Long-term storage: beyond a single run

Samples and reagents that outlive any single run go into dedicated long-term storage — refrigerators (4 °C), freezers (-20 °C or -80 °C), or in some cases liquid nitrogen, chosen based on how sensitive the material is and how long it needs to remain viable. Automated **storage systems** extend this into full robotic retrieval: racks or plates sit in a temperature-controlled vault, tracked by barcode, and a robotic picker retrieves a specific item on request without a person ever opening the door and disturbing the controlled environment for everything else stored alongside it.

### Environmental control as a continuation, not a separate concern

Storage and environmental control aren't a separate topic from everything else in this chapter — they're a continuation of it. The same barcode that identifies a plate during active processing is what a storage system uses to retrieve it later; the same sensitivity to temperature and light that shaped which vessel to choose is exactly what a storage system exists to manage while that vessel waits. Getting the active steps of a workflow right accomplishes nothing if the sample degrades in the gaps between them.
