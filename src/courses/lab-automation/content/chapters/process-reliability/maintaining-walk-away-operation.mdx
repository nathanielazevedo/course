Walk-away time was introduced back in the automated-laboratory chapter as one of automation's core benefits — a system running unattended for hours or overnight. Actually earning that unattended stretch, reliably, night after night, is a design discipline of its own.

### Walk-away time is a claim that has to be defended continuously

An overnight run isn't unattended because nothing could go wrong — it's unattended because everything that's *likely* to go wrong within that stretch has been anticipated and handled without a person. Every hour of claimed walk-away time is really a claim that the system can detect and either recover from or safely wait out any failure that occurs during that hour, using exactly the detection and recovery layers from the previous section. A system that claims 12 hours of walk-away time but actually needs a person to intervene after 4 doesn't have 12 hours of walk-away time — it has 4, and the rest was optimistic marketing.

### The resource budget for an unattended stretch

Every consumable a run depends on — tips, reagent volume, wash buffer — has to be provisioned for the *entire* unattended stretch up front, since there's no one available partway through to refill anything. This means walk-away duration and resource capacity are directly linked: extending an unattended run from 8 hours to 16 isn't just a scheduling change, it requires physically loading twice the tips, twice the reagent, and verifying the deck actually has room for that much supply before the run starts.

### Redundancy as a walk-away strategy

Where possible, systems extend safe walk-away duration by building in redundancy: a spare tip box loaded beyond the minimum needed, a backup reagent aliquot in case the primary one is contaminated, an alternate path for a step if its usual instrument reports an error. Redundancy trades deck space and consumable cost for a longer stretch of genuinely unattended operation — a direct extension of the resource-provisioning idea, aimed specifically at absorbing the unexpected rather than just the expected.

### The limit that redundancy can't remove

No amount of redundancy makes walk-away time unlimited. Some failures — a hardware fault that stops the arm entirely, a power interruption — simply cannot be worked around by any amount of spare consumables, and represent a genuine ceiling on how long a system can run without a person available to respond. Recognizing this ceiling honestly, rather than assuming a system can run indefinitely unattended, is part of realistic walk-away planning; a scheduled check-in before that ceiling is reached is a deliberate design choice, not a failure of the automation.

Walk-away operation is about surviving the *expected* stretch of unattended time without incident. The next section covers what happens when the system needs to actively tell a person something is wrong, rather than silently handling it alone.
