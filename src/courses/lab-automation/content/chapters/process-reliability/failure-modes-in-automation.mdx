A validated, well-designed method still runs on physical hardware, day after day, unattended. This chapter is about what keeps that unattended operation from quietly falling apart — starting with a clear-eyed look at how automated systems actually fail.

### Mechanical failures: the physical world wearing down

**Mechanical failure** covers anything physical breaking or degrading: a gripper losing grip force, a pipette's seals wearing out (the same drift covered back in the calibration section), a robotic arm's positioning drifting slightly out of tolerance over months of use. These failures are usually gradual — a system doesn't go from perfect to broken in one step, it degrades slowly until it crosses a threshold where results start being visibly affected, often well after that degradation actually began.

### Software failures: the logic breaking down

**Software failure** is when the controlling logic itself misbehaves — a scheduler miscalculating a timing window, a driver mishandling an unexpected response from an instrument, a bug that only appears under a specific, rare sequence of events. Unlike mechanical failure, software failure doesn't wear in gradually; it's either present in the code from the start (waiting for the right conditions to trigger it) or introduced suddenly by an update, which is why software changes deserve the same scrutiny as physical hardware changes.

### Consumable failures: running out or running wrong

**Consumable failure** happens when something the system depends on but doesn't own outright — tips, reagent volume, wash buffer — runs out mid-run, or turns out to be the wrong item entirely (an unlabeled reagent bottle swapped into the wrong position). This category was touched on directly in the tip-management and washer sections earlier in the course; it recurs here because it's one of the most common real-world causes of a failed run, precisely because it depends on correct human setup rather than anything the automated system controls on its own.

### Human-introduced failures: the setup step that didn't happen

A significant share of failures in every category above ultimately traces back to a human step upstream of the automated run itself: a plate loaded in the wrong orientation, a method file that wasn't updated to match a protocol change, a maintenance step that was skipped or done incorrectly. These aren't a separate technical category so much as a reminder that "automated" doesn't mean "no human involvement" — it means the human involvement moved earlier, into setup and maintenance, where a mistake is less visible in the moment but no less consequential later.

### Why categorizing failure modes matters

Knowing which category a failure belongs to shapes how it should be detected and prevented — a theme the rest of this chapter builds on directly. Mechanical failure is caught by monitoring for gradual drift before it crosses a threshold; software failure is caught by testing and careful change management rather than by watching for symptoms; consumable failure is caught by tracking supply state and verifying setup, not by inspecting the instrument itself. Treating every failure as one undifferentiated category of "something went wrong" makes it much harder to build the specific defenses each one actually needs.
