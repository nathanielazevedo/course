Everything else in this chapter is about handling failure gracefully once it happens. Maintenance is about preventing as much of it as possible from happening in the first place — the ongoing work that keeps a system's failure rate low enough that alerting and recovery stay the exception, not the routine.

### Preventive versus reactive maintenance

**Reactive maintenance** happens after something breaks — fix it once it's already failed. **Preventive maintenance** happens on a fixed schedule, regardless of whether anything currently seems wrong: replacing a seal before it wears out completely, recalibrating a pipette before it drifts out of tolerance, cleaning a wash line before residue accumulates enough to matter. Preventive maintenance costs time and consumables on a schedule whether or not a failure was imminent that particular week — a cost paid specifically to avoid the much larger, less predictable cost of an unattended run failing partway through.

### Why preventive maintenance connects directly to calibration and drift

This is the same logic from the calibration section back in the liquid-handling chapter, generalized to every wearing part in the system, not just the pipette. Mechanical failure, established earlier in this chapter, is usually gradual — which means a fixed maintenance schedule is really a bet that the schedule's interval is shorter than the time it typically takes for wear to become a real problem. Too long an interval, and preventive maintenance stops actually preventing anything; too short, and it wastes time and parts replacing things that still had useful life left.

### Uptime as the number that ties this whole chapter together

**Uptime** is the fraction of scheduled operating time a system is actually available and working — the single number that reflects how well failure modes, detection, walk-away planning, alerting, and maintenance are all working together in practice. A system with excellent detection and recovery but poor preventive maintenance still has low uptime, because it spends its time recovering from failures that better maintenance would have prevented outright. Uptime is a lagging indicator: it doesn't tell you which specific thing is wrong, but it tells you, honestly, whether everything in this chapter is actually working together or not.

### Maintenance windows compete with the same scheduling constraints as everything else

Performing maintenance takes an instrument offline, which means it competes for time against the throughput and walk-away goals from earlier in this course — the same scheduling tradeoffs covered in that chapter apply directly to deciding *when* maintenance happens, not just how often. Scheduling preventive maintenance during a planned downtime window, rather than interrupting a run in progress, is itself a scheduling decision with the same resource and timing considerations as any other step.

This closes the chapter on process reliability — the categories of failure, detecting and recovering from them, honestly planning for walk-away operation, alerting a person when it matters, and the preventive maintenance that keeps failures rare in the first place. The final chapter of this course turns to the broader framework that governs all of this in a regulated setting: quality, compliance, and validation.
