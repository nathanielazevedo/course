Lab automation is the use of machines to carry out the physical and informational steps of an experiment that a person would otherwise do by hand. It is not one device. It is a chain: move a liquid, move a plate, apply heat, read a signal, record the result.

### The unit of work

The atomic action in most automated labs is moving a precise volume of liquid from one container to another. Pipetting. Almost everything else — heating, mixing, washing, reading — happens to liquids that a machine placed somewhere on purpose.

A workflow is a sequence of these actions applied to **labware**: plates, tubes, and reservoirs that hold the samples and reagents. The machine does not understand the biology. It executes positions, volumes, and timings.

### Three layers

An automated lab is built in three layers, each depending on the one below it.

- **Hardware** — the physical devices that act on labware: liquid handlers, robotic arms, plate readers, incubators, centrifuges.
- **Control** — the software that drives each device. A driver turns a high-level command (*aspirate 50 µL from well A1*) into the device's own motor and valve operations.
- **Orchestration** — the scheduler that decides what runs when, moves labware between devices, and keeps the whole process coordinated.

A single pipette with a control program is automation. A bench of instruments tied together by a scheduler is an automated *system*. The difference is orchestration.

### Automation is not just speed

The obvious reason to automate is throughput — a machine pipettes a 384-well plate faster and more consistently than a human. But the deeper reasons are **reproducibility** and **walk-away time**. A machine repeats the exact same motion at 2 a.m. as it did at noon, and it does so without a person standing over it. Consistency and unattended operation often matter more than raw speed.

What automation does *not* do is think. It cannot notice that a reagent looks wrong or that a tip missed the well unless it was explicitly told how to detect and respond to that case. Everything the system handles, someone designed it to handle. That gap — between what a human would catch and what the machine was told to catch — is where most of the engineering in later chapters lives.
