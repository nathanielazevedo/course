Before any of it is automated, a lab workflow is a person, a pipette, and a protocol sheet. Understanding exactly where that setup breaks down is what justifies — and shapes — everything a machine is later asked to do.

### What a manual workflow looks like

A technician reads a protocol, sets a pipette to a volume, and moves liquid from a reagent trough into a plate, well by well. For a 96-well plate that's 96 repetitions of the same motion, done by hand, for one step of one plate. A full assay is a chain of dozens of such steps — dispense, incubate, wash, spin, read — each done manually before the next begins.

Nothing about this is wrong. It is exactly how the sciences behind these assays were developed, and a skilled technician can do it well. The problems only show up at scale, and they show up in the same handful of places every time.

### Where manual work breaks down

- **Variability.** No two manual pipetting motions are identical. The angle of the tip, the speed of the plunger, the exact moment of release all shift slightly well to well, and shift again when a different technician does the same protocol. That variability shows up in the data as noise that has nothing to do with the biology being measured.
- **Fatigue.** A technician pipetting the same motion for the two-hundredth time does not move quite like they did on the first. Accuracy and consistency degrade over the course of a long run, and the drift is rarely uniform — the last plate processed is not equivalent to the first.
- **Throughput.** A person can only move so fast, and only for so many hours a day. Scaling a manual workflow to more samples means scaling the number of people and hours, not just running the same process longer.
- **Hands-on time.** Every manual step requires a person physically present and attentive. That time cannot be reclaimed for other work, and it cannot happen unattended — no manual protocol runs itself overnight.

### The limits compound

These four problems are not independent. Variability and fatigue combine to make the *last* plate in a large manual run measurably less reliable than the first — and because a person is tied up the whole time, there's no way to simply add more throughput without adding more people making the same kinds of errors. A workflow that is merely slow when done manually becomes, at scale, a workflow that is also inconsistent.

None of this means people are worse at bench work than machines — for judgment calls, troubleshooting, and small-scale exploratory work, the opposite is often true. It means that repetitive, high-volume, precisely-timed work is exactly the profile a machine is suited to and a person is not. That is the gap the automated system in the next section is built to close.
